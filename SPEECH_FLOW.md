# TaskForce AI: the speech (Dixie Road & Mayfield Road)

*Read it straight through. Lines in quotes inside the speech are typed or said to TaskForce AI as you say them. The
reports are built live, so never say a number until it is on the screen: point, then read.*

---

Picture this. You're running the Dixie Road and Mayfield Road widening. Two roads, crews out every day, the Region
watching. It's Monday, seven a.m.

Over the weekend, the updates piled up. Daily site reports from the field. A schedule that moved. Costs landing in
finance. Extra work waiting on the client's sign-off.

Every one of them matters. None of them talk to each other. And the one question you actually have, *"are we okay"*,
isn't answered in any of them.

So you start calling. The project manager, the superintendent, accounting. By Thursday you have an answer. By then,
it's already cost you.

Now imagine you just ask. TaskForce AI goes through every one of those updates for you, and comes back with one page:
what's wrong, how bad, and what to do first. And every number on that page comes straight from your own records.

So let's do exactly that. Monday, seven a.m., one question:

**"What should I worry about on Dixie this week?"**

Watch what it's doing while it works. It's going through the schedule, the site reports, the money and the delays, all
at the same time. This is the part that normally takes your team the better part of a week.

And there it is. Up top, three lines: what's wrong, how bad, and what to do first. That's your whole Monday meeting,
before you've had your coffee. Below it, the tiles give you where the job stands right now. And look at the delays:
half of what's holding this job up came from outside. Other parties, not our crews. At the bottom, the map: Dixie Road
and Mayfield Road, exactly where the work is.

One page, from the live records, instead of three reports from three departments. So let's pull on each thread.

Start with the leak nobody ever puts in a report: crews being paid to wait.

**"Where are we paying for crews that aren't producing on Dixie?"**

Here it is, trade by trade. These are the crews standing by, and these are their hours. This line shows you when it
happened, month by month, and you can see exactly where it spiked. And these are the actual days, the actual site
reports, carrying the most standby. That's where the conversation with the superintendent starts this week.

Nobody hid this. It was sitting in the site reports the whole time. Nobody had the time to add it up.

Now the money.

**"How much money is the Dixie & Mayfield job owed right now, and what should we chase first?"**

That's the number stuck on this job right now, and most of it is work that's already in the ground, done, just not yet
approved for billing. And it doesn't stop at the total. It tells you which line to chase first, item by item, and
what to put in this month's progress claim. That's working capital sitting on site, and finding it took one question.
And when you want your project manager on it, this page goes straight to them as a PDF.

Now the delays, and which of them belong on a claim.

**"Which Dixie delays can we bill back to the Region?"**

Every delay on this job, logged by the site, with the date and the report it came from. An unmarked Bell duct bank.
Nesting birds in the clearing limits. An archaeological find. Each one written down the day it happened. That's the
evidence a claim is built on, and these are the ones worth taking to the Region. And here, on the map, is where it's
all happening.

Now, every answer you've just seen is only as good as what comes in from site. So let me show you the other end. This
is what a site supervisor does at the end of his day.

**"I want to fill today's daily site report."**

The report opens right here, beside the chat. No typing, no dropdowns. He just talks.

**"Start a daily report for Dixie Road and Mayfield Road, on the Dixie corridor site. Day shift, Gurpreet Sandhu
supervising."**

**"On item C-15 a, we had one open-cut foreman, four open-cut labourers and two pipelayers, ten hours each."**

**"On the same item, we ran the 40 tonne excavator and three tri-axle dump trucks, ten hours each."**

**"And we placed three hundred tonnes on C-15 a."**

**"We lost two hours waiting for Bell to locate a duct bank at station fourteen plus two-eighty."**

The crew, the equipment, the quantity against the contract item, and that Bell delay, each one matched to the real
records as he says it. And before he signs off, he asks one more thing:

**"What's still missing?"**

It checks the report, tells him what's left, and takes him straight to it.

**"Partly cloudy, twelve degrees."**

Done. And that two-hour Bell delay is now on the record, at a station, the same day it happened. That's the paper
trail that wins a delay claim. And the hours and quantities he just gave us are what feed tomorrow morning's standby
and billing answers.

So here's what this means for you.

You know where a job stands in one minute, not one week. You find idle crews and unbilled work while it's still
recoverable. And every delay is logged the same day it happens, ready for a claim.

You don't need more reports. You need fewer steps between the question and the answer.

Thank you.
